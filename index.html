Series proposal, second draft. Working title.

TheCheshireCat

Tap the cat

Short films about networks, mathematics, and the strange things that happen when people, diseases, and power lines are connected. The math is real, the stories are real, and the cat never lies about which is which.

October 2026. Pilot: “Why do your friends have more friends than you?”

What changed from the first draft

  • The pilot now bridges the theorem to the Harvard study honestly. That study picked random people and asked them to name friends, so the script now shows that sampling rule has its own guarantee, and the pilot figure has a third bar for it.
  • The math moment is one sentence and one equation. The variance term lives in the deep cut.
  • The majority-illusion toy is rebuilt so nobody has just one friend, and its bars report the right number.
  • The branching-process episode moves to slot 02 and joins the proof-of-concept set. It sits closest to the creator’s own epidemic work.
  • Every episode now carries a labeled anchor type. The old rewiring episode becomes a deep cut, and a new episode on how flu travels between cities takes its place.
  • The cat loses its stripes. The grin is the logo.
  • The production plan is sized for one creator, with a throughput test before anyone is hired.
  • Every article in the source list has a checked DOI. One draft URL had a typo, flagged below.

The idea

Take a question anyone can answer, show why the obvious answer can’t be right, and leave the viewer seeing their own world differently.

Each film borrows four moves. The question spirals like a Vsauce opener. The mathematics becomes visible the way it does in 3Blue1Brown, so intuition is earned rather than asserted. A documented study or event raises the stakes the way a Vox explainer does. And a small simulation lets the viewer watch the mechanism run, in the spirit of Complexity Explained.

The difference is the person making it. A network scientist can hold a short film to research standards: every number traced to a source, every graphic labeled as simulation or measurement, and every claim scoped to what was actually shown.

Recommendation: make three proof-of-concept shorts (episodes 01 to 03). Commit to the full ten only if the science holds up in review, viewers can state the main idea after one watch, and the second film takes half the time of the first.

How an episode moves

  1. The familiar. A question the viewer can answer on instinct.
  2. The contradiction. Something about that answer can’t be right.
  3. The toy. A tiny example isolates the mechanism.
  4. The reveal. One equation or theorem resolves the contradiction.
  5. The evidence. A real study or event, labeled as such, changes the stakes.
  6. The return. Back to the opening question, now seen differently.

Each film promises one change of mind the viewer could say in a sentence afterward. For the pilot: “the people I know are a biased sample of the world.” Some episodes have no real-world case to stand on. Those skip step 5 openly and say they are pure mathematics, rather than borrowing an event that doesn’t fit.

What gets made per episode

Short75 to 150 seconds, vertical first. The phenomenon, the mechanism, and one real consequence.
Deep cut3 to 5 minutes. The derivation, the counterexample, the caveats, and the paper.
InteractiveA slider-driven model on the notes site, like the one in the pilot section below.
Study cardOne page: derivation, assumptions, sources, and a self-test.

Pilot: why do your friends have more friends than you?

Target length 2 minutes 15 seconds. Built from original simulations, anchored in a 2009 campus flu study.

Look at the world through friendships and popular people show up over and over, because they sit at the end of many friendships. That bias is a nuisance when it warps what seems normal. It turned out to be useful in one study: friends named by randomly chosen students got the flu earlier, on average, than the students who named them.

The four claims, kept apart

Theorem 1: friendship ends

Pick a friendship at random, then one of its two ends. That person’s expected number of friends is at least the average person’s.

Theorem 2: friends of people

Pick a person at random, then one of their friends at random. Averaged over people, that friend also has at least as many friends as the average person. Different weights from Theorem 1, same direction.

Not a theorem: your own friends

“Most people have friends more popular than themselves” is common in real networks but not guaranteed by either result.

A measurement: one outbreak

In the Harvard study of 744 students, the nominated-friends group led by 13.9 days in fitted clinical-diagnosis curves. One campus, one season, observational.

Theorem 2 is the one the Harvard study actually used. The first draft went from Theorem 1 straight to the study, which a careful viewer would catch. Both theorems take one line each:

Theorem 1: E[K2]E[K] = E[K] + Var(K)E[K] ≥ E[K]
Theorem 2: 1n ∑i 1ki ∑j∼i kj = 1n ∑edges ( kikj + kjki ) ≥ 2mn = E[K]

Theorem 2 needs every person to have at least one friend, and uses x + 1/x ≥ 2. Equality in both holds only when everyone has the same number of friends. One more caveat goes on screen: real nominations aren’t random picks, since people name close friends. The study sampled that way, and the film says so.

The first season

Ten episodes in release order. The first three are the proof of concept.

Anchor labels: documented case a real event or experiment; published result a peer-reviewed analysis; pure math no real-world case, and the film says so.

  1. Why do your friends have more friends than you?

    Degree, size-biased sampling, two friendship-paradox theorems. About 2:15.

    Feld 1991; Christakis and Fowler 2010.

    proof of conceptdocumented case

  2. Why does a spark usually go nowhere?

    Branching processes, extinction probability, and why superspreading makes most outbreaks fizzle even when R0 is well above 1. About 1:55.

    Lloyd-Smith et al. 2005, on SARS and other real outbreaks.

    proof of conceptpublished result

  3. Can a minority look like a majority?

    Majority illusion, degree and attribute correlations. Deep cut: the same people with the same friend counts, rewired into a different society (degree-preserving swaps, assortativity). About 1:40.

    Lerman, Yan and Wu 2016; Newman 2002. Prentice and Miller 1993 as a documented case of students misjudging a campus norm, labeled as a real misperception, not proof that network structure caused it.

    proof of conceptpublished result

  4. Is the world actually small?

    Distance, clustering, shortcuts. Why the six-degrees story is half myth, and what a later email experiment found. About 1:35.

    Watts and Strogatz 1998; Dodds, Muhamad and Watts 2003.

    documented case

  5. When do random dots become a world?

    Erdős–Rényi graphs and the sudden giant component. About 1:45.

    Original simulation and classical theory.

    pure math

  6. Why does winning make you win more?

    Cumulative advantage, preferential attachment, heavy tails, and the live argument over how common scale-free networks really are. About 1:50.

    Merton 1968; Price 1976; Barabási and Albert 1999; Broido and Clauset 2019.

    published result

  7. How do you break a network?

    Random failure versus targeted attack on hubs. About 1:45.

    Albert, Jeong and Barabási 2000, with its published correction.

    published result

  8. Can you find groups that aren’t there?

    Modularity, null models, and why random graphs can look clustered. About 2:15.

    Zachary 1977, a karate club that really split in two; Guimerà, Sales-Pardo and Amaral 2004.

    documented casepublished result

  9. Why did the flu reach some cities first?

    Mobility networks and effective distance: on a map an outbreak looks random, but measured through air travel it spreads in rings. About 2:00.

    Brockmann and Helbing 2013, using 2009 H1N1 and 2003 SARS.

    published result

  10. Can one failure darken a continent?

    Interdependent networks and cascading failure. About 2:30.

    The 2003 North American blackout (Task Force report) and Buldyrev et al. 2010, which drew on a separate 2003 Italian blackout. The historical cases and the toy model stay visibly separate.

    documented casepublished result

The cat

A solid silhouette, two big eyes, and a grin that outlasts everything else. No stripes, so the character can’t be mistaken for a famous studio’s version.

The cat is punctuation, not a host. It appears in a lower corner when there is something to react to, and disappears during full-screen plots, dense citations, and delicate derivations. When an episode lands its main idea, the cat fades and the grin stays. That grin is the channel’s logo, end card, and favicon.

These four states are a placeholder sketch drawn for this draft. Final character sheets and the animation rig are still to be made.

Voice, picture, captions

Narration is the creator’s own voice, recorded in paragraph-sized takes from a full script, kept conversational. Natural cadence and honest uncertainty are part of the brand. Picture is 1080 × 1920 at 30 fps, action near the center, captions clear of platform overlays. Colors carry meaning across the whole series: aqua for structure and plain sampling, pink for the contradiction and the biased view, warm cream for the idea being selected.

Rigor as a feature

Standards a researcher can hold that most short-form channels don’t.

  • Claim ledger. Every number, date, causal claim, and universal statement is tied to a source and the exact wording it supports.
  • Label every picture. Each graphic is marked as original simulation, concept animation, reported study result, or real measurement.
  • Assumptions on screen. Finite or infinite, directed or undirected, which sampling rule, which model.
  • Counterexample first. Try the cheapest way to break a claim before celebrating it.
  • Real people only. No invented individuals; dated, documented events with the right permissions.
  • Three voices kept apart. “The authors measured,” “a toy model shows,” and “we think” are never blended.

Pilot red flags

Each of these is false or unsupported, and none may appear in the film:

  • “Everyone has less popular friends.”
  • “The Harvard friends were random friendship ends.” They were friends named by random students, which is Theorem 2, not Theorem 1.
  • “Watching your friends always warns you two weeks early.”
  • “The 2003 blackout followed our cascade animation.”

Making it with one person

The real constraint is the creator’s time, so the plan tests throughput before it spends money.

Week 1Claim ledger, both theorems checked against code, script draft, outside reader lined up.
Week 2Animatic: the 15-second hook and the star sequence, with scratch narration. Sound-off check.
Week 3Final narration, animation, sound, on-screen citations, deep-cut outline.
Week 4Outside review, captions, platform exports, notes page with the interactive, small audience test.

Tools. Manim Community for equations and the sampling sequences. Python and NetworkX for every graph and every number. D3 for the web interactives. One shared palette and graph-style module so episode 02 starts from episode 01’s parts.

Crew. The creator writes, narrates, animates, and signs off on the science. One outside network scientist reads each script, as a paid read or a swap. A motion designer comes in only if the throughput test fails.

Throughput test. Episode 02 should take at most half the hours of episode 01. If it doesn’t, the reusable parts aren’t reusable yet, and that gets fixed before episode 03.

Scorecard

  • Science: no material errors after outside review; every graphic keeps its label.
  • Understanding: a viewer can say the sampling bias in one sentence.
  • Picture: the hook makes sense with the sound off.
  • Audience: early retention, average watch time, saves, and confused comments, not views alone.

Small viewer test. Show 5 to 10 people with mixed backgrounds two rough cuts, one with the cat and one without. Ask them to state the main claim and whether the Harvard result came from a study or a simulation. This checks clarity, not market fit.
